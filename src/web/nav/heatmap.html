    Look

    How to read this

    Sectors. The game cuts the galaxy into sectors, each 1,280 light years wide, deep and high. The squares of the flat map are these sectors seen from above, but each square is really a stack of sectors, one on top of another.

    Layers. One level of that stack is a layer. The galaxy is flat like a dinner plate, about 90,000 light years across but only a few thousand thick, so known stars sit in at most six layers. Each keeps its own name wherever you are: Deep above, Far above, Just above the middle, Just below the middle, Far below and Deep below. The two middle layers meet 25 light years below Sol, close to the galaxy's middle, and hold about 95 of every 100 known systems. One layer further up or down, stars thin out and few commanders go: there are only a few known systems there for every hundred in the middle (around the bubble, about one). Part of that is real (there are fewer stars) and part is that nobody has looked. The strip beside the map shows the layers from the side, with a dot for you.

    Cubes. Inside each sector the game files its stars into smaller cubes, so a star's name is its address. In Synuefai YQ-A c15-1, "Synuefai" is the sector, "YQ-A c15" is which cube of 40 light years inside it, and "1" is the star's number in that cube. The small letter gives the cube's size: a is 10 light years, and each letter after it doubles that, up to h, a whole sector. When you look inside a sector, Studio breaks it into 512 cubes of 160 light years; 80 and 320 are a click away.

    Nebulae. Nebulae and star clusters such as California Sector are not sectors. They are spheres laid over the sectors, and the stars inside one take its name instead of the sector's. A padlock means the game asks for a permit to go in, which makes a nebula look cold: few may go there, not few have looked.

    The colours. Hot means busy: many known systems. Known means reported to community databases by players' tools, not discovered. A cube can look cold because nobody has uploaded what is there, because there are few stars there, or because a permit keeps people out. A sector's colour counts only the systems named after it (EDAstro's list), so stars inside a nebula and catalogue stars (HIP, HD and so on) are missing from it; a look inside counts every system Spansh knows there.

    Recorded and not recorded. Recorded is the share of a cube's known systems whose every body has been reported: fully recorded. A partly recorded system has had its discovery scan reported but not all of its bodies; a never scanned one has had no discovery scan reported at all. Not recorded counts those two, plus the systems that must exist but no database has: the stars of each small cube are numbered from 0 up, so a gap in the numbers is a system nobody has reported. Your first discoveries counts the systems your own journal shows you were first to scan.

    Moving around. The camera always turns round one point, the small faint cross. Drag with the left mouse button to turn round it, all the way round and up or down; drag with the right (or middle) button to slide it; the wheel zooms towards the pointer. W and S go forward and back the way you look (level, while Hold height is on), A and D left and right, R and F (or E and Q) up and down; Shift doubles the speed. The Camera button above the map opens a pad that does all this with the mouse alone (hold a button down to keep going), with West, North, South and East, which move the map one sector, and Up and Down, which show the next layer.

    Choosing. A click on a sector, a cube, a star, a nebula or one of your finds chooses it and centres the camera on it; a double-click goes closer; a click on empty space does nothing. What is chosen, the highlight, is outlined in white over everything in front of it. Click the same spot again (slowly: two quick clicks are a double-click) to reach the cube or sector behind it, and again for the next one, all the way through and round to the first; the camera stays put meanwhile, so the outline steps back behind the cubes in front. Over a sector's cubes, a click on the edge of a nebula's sphere takes the cube: the nebula comes after everything else on that line. The arrow keys (and the arrows on its card) move the highlight one cube, or one sector, at a time: left and right, away and towards, as you see the map; Page Up and Page Down move it up and down. The camera stays where it is, and C centres the map on the highlight; one moved off the map has its label at the map's edge, pointing to it. Over a sector's edge the highlight goes on into the next sector's cubes when Studio has them on this PC, else onto that sector. Cube level, under the map, shows one level of a sector's cubes alone, so the cubes inside can be clicked.

    Flat views. View switches between 3D and two flat views of the same map. Top (2D) looks straight down, north up and east right, like the flat map. Side (2D) looks from the south towards the core, up up and east right, so the layers stand one above the other as on the strip beside the map. In the flat views the camera cannot turn: any drag slides the map, and so do W, A, S and D. Clicks choose and centre as in 3D, and the arrow keys move the highlight: from above, up is north; from the side, up is up.

    Markers. The orange ring is you, with a dashed line down to the galaxy's middle to show your height. The cyan ring is the system the game has targeted, and the cyan square the end of your plotted route; one placed from its name says so, with how far off it may be, and its cube is outlined. Green diamonds are your finds. Small dots joined by a faint line are the stops of your plotted route, coloured as the Route check on the left colours them: grey waiting, blue being checked, green recorded, gold not recorded, red not checked. The faint orange ball round you is your jump range (Within reach, under the map), with a dot for each system in it in the same colours: a paler green is recorded by its name only, and a hollow ring is a system that must exist but no database has. The Markers switch hides them all; Nebulae shows every nebula, only those near the point the camera turns round, or none.

    Within reach and Nav scan. Within reach lists, after every jump, the systems your ship can reach with its next one: from the detailed map on this PC where it covers your place, else with one request to Spansh. Nav scan reads the system names in each screenshot you take while it is on (F10, or Steam's own screenshot key), checks each, pings when one is not recorded, and lists them. A name read off a picture can be misread, so target it in the game to confirm it. Both use Studio's memory of checks: a recorded system is remembered for the days you choose, a not recorded one for 24 hours, and "Forget all checks" has every system checked afresh.

    Names to try. The game numbers the systems of each of its cubes from 0 up, with no gaps. Under the map, each cube's known numbers are listed with numbers to try: a gap below the highest known must exist, and the next three may exist. Copy one and paste it into the galaxy map's search: if the game finds it and no database has it, it is not recorded yet. Target it or plot a route to it and Studio checks it for you, and keeps it in Your finds.

    Sector counts come from the sector list of EDAstro (edastro.com, by CMDR Orvidius), and nebula names from EDAstro's nebula list and the community nebula catalogue it is built from; Studio fetches each only when you ask. The nebula spheres and the reading of star names into places are from EDTS by Esvandiary and Andy Martin (BSD-3 licence), after CMDR Jackie Silver's work on the names. The systems inside a sector or a nebula are counted and listed by Spansh (spansh.co.uk), one request at a time and only when you click, and kept on this PC. The colours follow matplotlib's Inferno (CC0) and the ED3D galaxy map (MIT licence). All are free, made or run by players for all of us.
